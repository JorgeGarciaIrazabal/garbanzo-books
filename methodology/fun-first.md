# Fun First — the north star

Read this before every story. It outranks every other doc in this folder. When any
other rule (reading level, interaction "skills", a tidy theme) fights *fun*, fun wins.

## What we are actually making

We make books a kid **begs to read again** — and begs a grown-up to read *one more page*
at bedtime. The win condition is simple and brutal:

> A 6-year-old grabs the next page before you finish this one.

We are **not** in the lesson business. We are not making "books that teach X while being
fun." We are making books that are *gloriously fun*, and if a kid happens to get better at
reading because they couldn't put it down — great, that's the side effect, never the goal.

The competition isn't other worthy storybooks. It's the tablet, the cartoon, the candy.
Write like you have to win attention back from *Dog Man* and *Bluey*, because you do.

## The register: bold & mischievous

Think **Roald Dahl, Maurice Sendak, Mo Willems, Dav Pilkey, Adventure Time.** That means:

- **Real stakes & real peril.** Something can actually go wrong. A monster, a chase, a
  trapdoor, a plan that blows up. Cozy is fine *between* the scary-fun bits, never instead
  of them.
- **Mischief is the engine.** Heroes who scheme, break rules, talk back, make a glorious
  mess — and are *not* tidily punished for it. Naughty is funny. Naughty is relatable.
- **Be funny on purpose.** Gross-out, slapstick, absurdity, a character who is wrong in a
  hilarious way, a running gag that pays off, a deadpan narrator. Aim for at least one
  real laugh per spread for the younger bands.
- **Surprise.** Subvert the obvious next beat. The "safe" guess should be wrong in a
  delightful way. Twist endings, fake-outs, the smallest character saving the day.
- **A voice with attitude.** Narrators and characters who are cheeky, dramatic, sarcastic,
  over-the-top. Flat "and then they were happy" prose is the enemy.
- **Momentum.** Every page yanks you to the next. No page exists to "establish" or to
  "reflect." If a kid could stop here without caring, cut it.

## The hard ban list (what makes our old books boring)

Do not do these. They are why the current catalogue feels soft and preachy:

- ❌ **The moral-of-the-story ending.** No character pausing to explain what they learned.
  No "and that's how Pip discovered that sharing is caring." End on the *funniest* or most
  *satisfying* image, then stop.
- ❌ **The lesson disguised as a plot.** If you can summarise the book as "a story that
  teaches kids to ___," start over. The plot is not a delivery vehicle for a value.
- ❌ **Sanded-down, conflict-free niceness.** Everyone polite, nothing at risk, problems
  that solve themselves gently. Boring. Give it teeth.
- ❌ **Earnest, therapy-speak feelings.** Kids feel things through *action and slapstick*,
  not through characters narrating their emotions in soft, careful language.
- ❌ **Defanging the mischief.** Don't have the naughty hero apologise and reform on the
  last page. Let them get away with it (or get a funnier comeuppance, not a moral one).
- ❌ **Telegraphic robot-prose.** Fragment-chains written to duck a readability number:
  "Seoul at night. Bright lights. Palaces glow. Best snack spot." No subjects, no verbs,
  no flow — nobody *tells a story* like that, so it has no voice and no momentum. Short
  is great; *amputated* is dead. Write flowing sentences that connect (and, but, so,
  then, because) and pass the read-aloud test. See "the telegraphic trap" in
  `reading-pedagogy.md`.

## The one line of decency (this is NOT "PC")

There is exactly one taste rule, and it is small: **don't punch down.** Don't build the
joke on lazy stereotypes of real groups of people. That's it. Villains, danger, gross
stuff, rule-breaking, anarchy, mild scares — all fully welcome. This rule exists to keep
the humour *smart*, not to sand off a single edge. If you ever feel the urge to make a
story "safer," you have almost certainly misread this section — re-read the top.

## Fun levers (reach for these when a page goes flat)

1. **Raise the stakes** — make the consequence bigger, closer, or sillier-but-dire.
2. **Add a ticking clock** — they have until the soup boils / the moon rises / Gran wakes.
3. **Make the hero scheme** — a ridiculous plan with moving parts the reader can enjoy.
4. **Plant a running gag** — something that comes back three times, bigger each time.
5. **Give the narrator opinions** — let the telling itself be funny.
6. **Break the obvious beat** — whatever the reader expects next, do the funnier opposite.
7. **End the spread on a cliff** — a threat, a question, a "uh oh" — so the page *must* turn.

## How this reframes the rest of the pipeline

- **Structure (story-craft):** the spine still matters — but it serves the fun, it doesn't
  replace it. A well-built skeleton with no jokes and no jeopardy is a dead book.
- **Language (age-fit):** match words to what a kid that age can read so nothing blocks the
  story. That's a *light touch*, not a curriculum. Never trade a great word or a great
  joke to hit a readability number.
- **Interactions:** they're *games* — a fun break that's part of the romp — not hidden
  reading drills. If it feels like a worksheet, cut it.
- **Art:** bold, dynamic, funny, a little dramatic. Not every world has to be soft pastel
  cozy; let danger look dangerous and let the gags read in the picture.
